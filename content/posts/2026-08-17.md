AI is still being sold as a productivity revolution, but the labor market story this month is more complicated: major companies are pairing record AI spending with workforce cuts, while official data still shows layoffs are historically low overall. That tension is exactly why Universal Basic Income remains part of the policy conversation.

---

### Key Stories

- **Oracle signals another round of cuts after years of AI-driven restructuring**
  Oracle is reportedly planning more layoffs only weeks after consuming most of a $2.1 billion restructuring budget, on top of about 21,000 jobs already eliminated over the past year. For labor markets, this is another sign that AI investment is increasingly being financed through organizational flattening and headcount reduction rather than purely through new hiring.  
  [Oracle plans more layoffs weeks after spending most of its $2.1 billion restructuring budget, report claims](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/oracle-plans-more-layoffs-weeks-after-spending-most-of-its-2.1-billion-restructuring-budget)

- **Microsoft’s July layoffs show how AI spending and job cuts now travel together**
  Microsoft said on July 6 that it was eliminating about 4,800 roles, or 2.1% of its workforce, while continuing to pour money into AI infrastructure and tools. The UBI relevance is straightforward: if even highly profitable firms are using AI-era restructuring to reduce labor costs, workers need stronger income backstops when the gains from automation are unevenly distributed.  
  [The latest in our company transformation](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/07/06/the-latest-in-our-company-transformation/)

- **Weekly jobless claims remain low, but they are still the clearest official early-warning signal**
  The Labor Department reported 209,000 initial unemployment claims for the week ending August 8, 2026, and AP noted that layoffs remain historically healthy even as companies keep announcing targeted cuts. That means the immediate labor shock is still contained, but it also suggests the risk is a slower, structural reshaping of work that could leave displaced workers scrambling before the macro data looks bad.  
  [US unemployment claims rise but remain at healthy level](https://apnews.com/article/ea0215d19c35367630cb637544e27748)

---

### What This Tells Us

The latest evidence points to a labor market that is still stable on paper but increasingly fragile underneath: firms are using AI to justify restructuring, while official layoffs data has not yet fully caught up. That is the basic case for UBI: if automation keeps shifting the balance between capital and labor faster than wages and retraining can respond, a guaranteed income floor becomes less ideological and more practical.
