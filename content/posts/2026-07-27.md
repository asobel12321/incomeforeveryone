AI-driven restructuring is no longer a theoretical risk; it is showing up in real headcount cuts, reorganizations, and shifting job demand. This week’s most relevant labor signals come from company announcements and fresh research on how automation is changing hiring, displacement, and the policy debate over income support.

---

### Key Stories

- **Amazon trims jobs in its AGI group as AI spending keeps climbing**
  Amazon cut jobs in its artificial general intelligence group on July 22, another sign that AI investment is being paired with selective labor shedding and team reshaping rather than broad-based hiring. For workers, the message is that even the units building AI can be exposed to restructuring; for UBI advocates, it strengthens the case for income support that is not tied to a single employer or occupation. [Amazon cuts jobs in its artificial general intelligence group](https://www.reuters.com/technology/amazon-cuts-jobs-in-its-artificial-general-intelligence-group-2026-07-22/)

- **Thomson Reuters says AI deployment is driving engineering cuts and a smaller, AI-native workforce**
  Thomson Reuters said on July 13 it was cutting a small number of engineering roles while pushing AI across its businesses, and Reuters reported the company expects to hire more than 250 net-new engineering roles over the next two years, mostly senior and “AI-native.” That mix of layoffs plus re-hiring in different skill categories is exactly the kind of workforce restructuring that can leave mid-career workers stranded even when headline employment does not collapse. [Thomson Reuters to cut 'small number' of engineering jobs](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/thomson-reuters-cut-small-number-engineering-jobs-2026-07-13/)

- **ILO pushes back on UBI as the only answer, even as AI displacement fears intensify**
  The International Labour Organization published a May 7 brief arguing that universal basic income is not the answer to AI’s labor-market effects, instead emphasizing jobs, productivity, and income support through a broader policy mix. The significance for this debate is not that UBI is rejected, but that serious institutions now treat AI displacement as real enough to require active policy design rather than passive adjustment. [Why Universal Basic Income is not the answer to the impact of AI on the labour market](https://www.ilo.org/publications/why-universal-basic-income-not-answer-impact-ai-labour-market)

---

### What This Tells Us

The labor story of 2026 is becoming clearer: AI is not just automating tasks, it is reorganizing teams, changing the mix of skills employers want, and putting pressure on workers whose jobs are easiest to redesign or eliminate. That makes the UBI conversation less abstract, because the policy question is no longer whether displacement could happen, but how to protect household income while the labor market is rebuilt around automation.
