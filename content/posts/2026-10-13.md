California’s new AI and labor tracker is a notable shift from debate to measurement: the state is now trying to see whether workers coming into unemployment insurance have come from occupations more exposed to AI. At the same time, the Labor Department’s latest guidance changes the minimum weekly Disaster Unemployment Assistance floor for the fourth quarter, a reminder that income support rules still matter when work is disrupted.

---

### Key Stories

- **California opens an AI exposure claims tracker**
  The state’s EDD has launched an official dashboard linking unemployment claims to the AI exposure level of claimants’ prior occupations, with a monthly release schedule and the next update due November 12. For labor and UBI watchers, the important development is not a headline layoff count but the creation of a live administrative signal that could show whether AI risk is concentrating in specific job types.
  [AI and the Labor Market](https://edd.ca.gov/aitracker)

- **Elon survey finds deep anxiety about AI and work**
  A new October 13 survey release finds 83% of U.S. adults expect AI to eliminate more jobs than it creates, while 65% say it could erode purpose and dignity by making work less meaningful. That matters for UBI because the public response is no longer just about displacement risk; it also reflects concern that the labor market may fail to provide stable income and social value.
  [Elon survey finds Americans fear AI will hollow out the meaning of work](https://www.elon.edu/u/news/2026/10/13/elon-survey-finds-americans-fear-ai-will-hollow-out-the-meaning-of-work/)

- **DOL updates disaster jobless-aid floor**
  The Labor Department issued UIPL No. 16-26 on October 6, transmitting the quarterly minimum weekly Disaster Unemployment Assistance amounts for disasters declared from October 1 through December 31, 2026. While not an AI policy, it is a concrete reminder that when shocks hit workers, the adequacy of cash support can be the difference between short disruption and lasting hardship.
  [UIPL No. 16-26](https://www.dol.gov/index.php/agencies/eta/advisories/uipl-no-16-26)

### What This Tells Us

Today’s evidence points to a more measurable phase of AI-related labor risk: California is starting to track displacement through claims data, public opinion is showing broad fear that AI will reduce both jobs and meaning, and the safety net still relies on benefit rules that were not designed for technology-driven churn. That combination makes the UBI question more concrete, because the debate is shifting from abstract job-loss scenarios to whether income support can keep pace with restructuring already visible in administrative data and worker sentiment.
