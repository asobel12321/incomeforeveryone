Canada’s newest evidence on AI and work is less dramatic than the layoff headlines, but it is important: Statistics Canada says it does not yet see a broad labor-market shock from generative AI. The clearest signal today is that policy responses are shifting toward preparedness and targeted upskilling, not panic.

---

### Key Stories

- **Canada sees no broad AI hiring collapse**
  Statistics Canada’s new analysis finds no substantial decrease in hiring or increase in job separations attributable to generative AI so far, including among younger workers and more exposed occupations. For UBI watchers, the near-term takeaway is not mass displacement, but a warning that labor effects may emerge unevenly and later than expected.
  [Hiring and job separation rates in the era of generative artificial intelligence: Early evidence](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/36-28-0001/2026010/article/00003-eng.htm)

- **StatCan builds a live AI labor evidence base**
  The agency also posted the catalog entry for its new article, anchoring the analysis to Labour Force Survey data from January 2006 through August 2026. That matters because the current debate over automation and income support now has a dated official benchmark instead of only forecasts and anecdotes.
  [Statistics Canada adds early evidence on AI exposure, hiring, and job separation](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/en/catalogue/36280001202601000003)

- **UK funds AI skills for frontline workers**
  On November 3, the UK government announced more than £800,000 for four Barnsley projects aimed at practical AI training for care workers, builders, women returning to work, young people, and small businesses. The specific implication is that governments are treating AI less as an abstract future shock and more as a workforce transition that needs immediate skill-building.
  [Care workers, women returning to employment and builders to gain practical AI skills](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/care-workers-women-returning-to-employment-and-builders-to-gain-practical-ai-skills)

### What This Tells Us

Today’s evidence points to a cautious middle ground: Canada’s official data does not show a broad AI-induced hiring break yet, but governments are still spending to prepare workers for a more uneven adjustment. That combination supports a UBI argument focused less on immediate collapse than on insurance against delayed, sector-specific disruption.
