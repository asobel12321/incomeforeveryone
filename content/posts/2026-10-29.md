The latest evidence points to an AI labor market that is expanding in some places while leaving workers to adapt on their own. UK government data show AI companies added jobs in 2025, but a separate worker survey says training is still not keeping pace with day-to-day use.

---

### Key Stories

- **UK AI sector jobs jump to 130,400**
  The UK government says AI-related employment in UK AI companies rose from about 86,000 in 2024 to about 130,400 in 2025, while AI revenues and gross value added also increased. For labor markets, that means AI is creating visible demand in a specific sector even as the broader question remains whether it will add more jobs than it displaces elsewhere.
  [Artificial Intelligence sector study 2025 (web version)](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/artificial-intelligence-sector-study-2025/artificial-intelligence-sector-study-2025-web-version)

- **Half of AI adopters report productivity gains**
  In the UK government’s AI adoption survey executive summary, half of AI adopters said they saw productivity gains, with task automation and workflow optimization the most common benefits. That suggests employers are still using AI mainly to reshape workflows and squeeze more output from existing teams, which raises the policy question of how displaced or restructured workers will be supported.
  [AI adoption survey report 2026: executive summary](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-survey-2026/ai-adoption-survey-report-2026-executive-summary)

- **Workers use AI widely, but training trails**
  ZipRecruiter’s 2026 AI worker report says 82.5% of workers use AI at work, yet only 28.9% say they get enough training and 37.2% are teaching themselves. That gap matters for job-market risk because it shows the burden of adaptation is landing on employees first, not on formal workforce systems.
  [Better Work, More to Learn: The 2026 AI Worker Report](https://www.ziprecruiter-research.org/economic-insights-research/2026-ai-worker-report)

---

### What This Tells Us

Today’s evidence is less about a sudden layoff wave than about an uneven restructuring process: AI-linked jobs are growing quickly in one part of the economy, while most workers are being pushed to absorb the technology without enough training. That combination strengthens the case for policy tools that cushion income risk and help workers move through transitions faster if automation keeps spreading.
